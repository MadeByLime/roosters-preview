Water doesn't wait.

A washer moves more water through your floor than anything else in the house, and a laundry room is rarely the lowest point in the building. A slow leak today is warped flooring and a call from the unit downstairs by next month. A puddle is the one thing here worth picking up the phone about the same day you see it.

Here's what usually goes.

Six faults that cover most of the washer calls we take. Start where we'd start. If the check comes up clean, the next step is ours.

  • Water won't drain

    The cycle ends with the drum still full.

    Try this first. Run a drain or spin-only cycle and listen. A pump that hums without moving water is blocked. Pull the machine out and check the drain hose for a kink, and make sure it sits no more than six inches into the standpipe, because pushing it deeper siphons and stalls. On a front-loader, open the small panel at the bottom front, set a towel and a shallow pan under it, and unscrew the filter. Coins, hair ties and a startling amount of lint live in there.

    Filter clear, hose straight, still full? Then it is the pump motor, the hose between the tub and the pump, or a board that never called for the drain. That one is a visit.

  • Clothes come out soaking

    The spin runs, but the load is still dripping.

    Try this first. Most of the time it is the load. One heavy item, a bath mat or a comforter, throws the drum off balance and the machine backs the spin off to protect itself. Spread the load and run the spin again. Check that the lid or door latch clicks hard, because a latch the machine cannot read means no high spin at all. Push each corner, and if it rocks, wind the feet down until it sits solid.

    Load spread, feet solid, still dripping? Now it is the drive belt, the motor coupler, the clutch or the lid switch, and those get measured rather than guessed at.

  • Water on the floor

    A puddle under the machine during a cycle, or after it.

    Try this first. Find where it starts. Pull the washer out and feel both inlet hoses at the valve and at the wall, since the small rubber washers inside those couplings go hard and weep. Then look at the detergent. An HE machine wants about a tablespoon, and more than that foams up and out the door. On a front-loader, peel the rubber door boot back and run a finger round the fold for grit, a coin or a split in the rubber.

    Hoses dry, detergent right, and it still puddles? The leak is behind the panel: tub seal, pump housing or the outer tub. Worth catching before the floor does.

  • It bangs and walks

    The machine shakes hard or moves across the floor on spin.

    Try this first. On a machine installed in the last month, check the shipping bolts came out of the back panel. Left in, they destroy the suspension. On anything older it is levelling. Adjust the feet until the cabinet will not rock in any direction, then tighten the lock nuts up against the body. If it sits on a wood floor over a crawl space, a sheet of plywood underneath does more than any adjustment will.

    Level, loaded evenly, and still banging? Worn suspension rods or shock absorbers. A roar that climbs with the spin speed is the drum bearing, and that one gets worse fast.

  • It won't start

    No lights, no fill, or it dies partway through a cycle.

    Try this first. Test the outlet with something else, then check the breaker and any GFCI in the laundry room. Open both faucets at the wall all the way, because a washer that cannot fill will sit there doing nothing at all. Unplug it for a full minute and start a short cycle, which clears a hung control board more often than you would expect. Write down any error code on the display before you reset it.

    Dead after that, or stopping at the same point every time? Door lock, inlet valve or main board. Give us the error code on the phone and we usually know what we are bringing.

  • It smells musty

    A sour smell in the drum, or on clothes straight out of it.

    Try this first. A front-loader seals tight, so whatever is left in it never dries. Wipe the fold in the door gasket and the glass behind it, pull the detergent drawer right out and rinse it, then run the tub clean cycle hot with a washer cleaner or bleach. Cut back to the HE dose, and leave the door open between loads. A month of that clears most of them.

    Cleaned, dosed right, and it still sours? There is biofilm in the outer tub or the sump where no cleaner reaches, or the drain is holding water it should have let go.

The questionswe get every week.

Nearly every washer fault comes down to water or balance. These are the ones homeowners ask us about before they book.

Is my washer worth repairing?

A drain pump, a door lock, a belt, a set of suspension rods or an inlet valve is almost always worth fixing. A failed drum bearing or a cracked outer tub on a budget front-loader usually is not, because the labor alone runs past what the machine is worth. We tell you which one you have before you spend anything past the diagnostic.

How long does a washer repair take?

Some are finished on the first visit. The van carries a small stock of the parts that fail most often, and a blocked filter or a split hose needs no part at all. When a part has to be ordered it takes three to five business days, then a return trip. You will know which it is before the tech leaves.

Can you work on a stacked set in a closet?

Yes, and that is most of what we see in San Francisco. The tech brings the unit out, works on it, and puts it back where it was. Clear a path to the closet and make sure the shutoff valves are reachable, and that is everything we need from you.

What does the diagnostic visit include?

A flat $140 covers the trip and the full diagnosis. The machine is run through fill, drain and spin, the leak path and drain line are checked, and the error history comes off the control board. You get the cause and a repair price on the spot, and the $140 comes off the labor if you go ahead.

Is the repair guaranteed?

Ninety days, covering the parts and the labor both. Parts are genuine manufacturer parts from licensed suppliers, nothing aftermarket. If the same fault turns up again inside that window, we come back and you pay nothing more.

Why are my clothes still soaked after the spin?

Usually the drain rather than the spin. A machine that cannot empty will never reach full spin speed, so from the laundry basket the two faults look identical. Check the drain filter first. If it is clear, the pump or the lid switch is next, and that is ours.

My supply hoses are the original rubber ones. Does that matter?

It matters more than the washer does. Burst inlet hoses are one of the most common sources of water damage in a home, and rubber hardens and splits with age. Braided stainless hoses cost very little and get replaced about every five years. Mention yours when you call and we will look at them while we are there.

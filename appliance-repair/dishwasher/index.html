You see this one late.

A washer floods a floor where you notice it. A built-in dishwasher drains into the cabinet base and the subfloor underneath, behind a toe-kick nobody takes off. By the time a dark line shows along the floor or the cabinet swells, it has usually been running for weeks. That is the one to look under rather than wait out.

Where it usually goes wrong.

Six faults that cover most dishwasher calls. Start with the check under each one, because on a dishwasher more of these work than you would expect. Where they run out, the panel says so.

  • Dishes come out dirty

    Grit, film or dried-on food left after a full cycle.

    Try this first. Pull the bottom rack out and look at the floor of the tub. Most machines have a cylinder filter there that twists out by hand, and if yours has never been cleaned it is full. Rinse it under the tap with a brush. Then spin both spray arms with a finger to check they turn freely, and hold them up to the light: the small jets block with seeds, labels and bits of glass, and a pin clears them. Last, run the kitchen tap until it comes through hot before you start the cycle, so the machine is not warming cold water on its own.

    Filter clean, arms spinning, hot water at the tap, and still dirty? Then the wash pump is not building pressure or the machine is not filling properly. That is a visit.

  • Water sits in the bottom

    An inch of standing water left in the tub when the cycle ends.

    Try this first. Scoop the water out with a cup, lift the filter and feel around the sump for glass, a fruit sticker or a chip of bone. If a garbage disposal was fitted recently, that is your first suspect: a new disposal ships with a knockout plug in the dishwasher inlet, and if the installer left it in the machine has nowhere to drain. Check the drain hose loops up high under the sink before it goes down, and if there is an air gap on the counter, pop the cap and clean it out.

    Sump clear, plug out, hose looped, and it still holds water? Drain pump or a blocked line inside the machine, and the unit comes out of the cabinet for that.

  • Water on the kitchen floor

    A puddle at the toe-kick, or drips down the front of the door.

    Try this first. Check what went in the dispenser first. Dish soap in a dishwasher makes enough foam to push water straight out of the door seal, and that is a surprisingly common call. If the detergent is right, run a finger round the door gasket for food, grease or a split, and wash it with warm soapy water. Then make sure the racks are not loaded so the door cannot close square, and put a level on the open door to check the machine is sitting flat in its opening.

    Right detergent, sound gasket, level machine, still wet? The leak is at the pump seal, the inlet valve or the tub itself, and all three live under the unit.

  • Everything comes out wet

    Plates and plastics still dripping when the cycle finishes.

    Try this first. Fill the rinse aid reservoir, and keep it filled. It is not an upsell, it breaks the surface tension so water sheets off instead of beading, and an empty one is the usual reason for wet plastic. Choose the heated dry option rather than eco if your machine offers both. Then crack the door open as soon as the cycle ends and leave it for twenty minutes, because most of the drying on a modern machine happens from the heat still in the load.

    Rinse aid topped up, heated dry on, and the load is still soaked? The heating element or the vent fan has gone, and those are both behind the panel.

  • It won't start

    No lights, no fill, or it stops partway through a cycle.

    Try this first. Push the door until the latch clicks, since a latch the machine cannot read leaves it dead. Then look for a control lock or a delay start left on by accident, which on most panels is a button held for three seconds. Check the breaker, and check the switch on the wall by the sink, because a lot of kitchens run the dishwasher off the same switch as the disposal. If the panel lights but nothing happens, cut the power at the breaker for a full minute to clear the board.

    Latched, unlocked, powered, and still nothing? Door latch switch, thermal fuse or the control board. The error code on the display tells us a lot before we arrive.

  • It smells

    A stale or sour smell when you open the door.

    Try this first. Start under the sink. The drain hose has to rise up high before it drops to the trap, and when that loop is missing or has sagged, sink water runs back into the machine and sits there. Fix the loop and most smells go with it. Then clean the filter, wipe the gasket fold, and run an empty cycle on the hottest setting with a dishwasher cleaner or a cup of white vinegar on the top rack.

    Hose looped, filter clean, hot cycle run, and it still sours? There is biofilm in the sump below the filter where nothing you pour in reaches, or the drain line is partly blocked.

Asked at thekitchen counter.

Plenty of what gets called a broken dishwasher turns out to be a filter, a spray arm or water going in cold. Here is the rest of what people ask us.

Is my dishwasher worth repairing?

A door latch, a gasket, a heating element, a drain pump or an inlet valve is almost always worth fixing. A failed control board on a budget machine often is not, because the part alone can run past half the price of a new unit, and we will tell you that instead of selling you the repair.

How long does a dishwasher repair take?

Many are done on the first visit. The van carries a small stock of the parts that fail most often, and a blocked sump or a jammed spray arm needs no part at all. When a part has to be ordered it takes three to five business days, then a return trip. You will know which one before the tech leaves.

Do you work on built-in dishwashers?

That is most of what we see. A built-in is plumbed, wired and bolted to the underside of the counter, so the water gets shut off at the stop valve under the sink and the machine comes out on its feet to be worked on. It goes back in levelled and secured, and we check the door swings clean against the cabinet before we leave.

What does the diagnostic visit include?

A flat $140 covers the trip and the full diagnosis. The tech runs a cycle and checks the fill, the wash pressure, the heat and the drain, inspects the sump, the seals and the hose under the sink, and reads any error codes off the board. You get the cause and a repair price on the spot, and the $140 comes off the labor if you go ahead.

Is the repair guaranteed?

Ninety days, covering the parts and the labor both. Parts are genuine manufacturer parts from licensed suppliers, nothing aftermarket. If the same fault turns up again inside that window, we come back and you pay nothing more.

Why are my glasses cloudy?

There is a test for this. Soak one in white vinegar for fifteen minutes. If the film lifts, it is hard water mineral deposit and a rinse aid plus a descaling cycle will handle it. If the glass still looks hazy, that is etching, which is the glass surface itself worn by too much detergent and very hot water, and nothing brings it back. Knowing which one you have saves you a service call.

Do I need to rinse the dishes before loading?

Scrape them, do not rinse them. Modern detergents are enzyme based and need some food to work on, so plates that went in already spotless often come out worse than plates that did not. Get the bones and the big scraps off, leave the rest, and skip the pre-rinse cycle on the sink.
